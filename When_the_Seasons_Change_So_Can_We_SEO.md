**When the Seasons Change, So Can We**

*Why shorter days can affect our mood \- and how to take care of ourselves through the darker months*

There is something magical about the first signs of fall. The air gets crisp, the leaves begin to change, sweaters come out of the closet, and suddenly everything feels a little cozier. We open the windows, decorate the porch, make soup, and enjoy those first cool evenings after a long summer. For a little while, the changing season feels like a welcome reset.

**And then the leaves fall.**

The trees become bare. The sun disappears earlier and earlier. The temperature drops, the days feel shorter, and cold weather begins keeping us indoors. The walks after dinner happen less often. Weekends can become quieter. For many of us, there comes a point in winter when we are leaving for work in the dark and coming home in the dark.

Sometimes, almost without noticing it, our mood begins to change with the season too.

The change is not imaginary. Light plays an important role in the body's internal clock, including the rhythms that help regulate sleep and wakefulness. As daylight decreases, some people notice changes in energy, sleep, appetite, motivation and mood. For some, those changes become significant enough to develop a seasonal pattern of depression, often referred to as seasonal affective disorder, or SAD. For many others, it may simply feel like being a little more tired, less motivated, more withdrawn or just not quite like ourselves.

But daylight is only part of the story. Winter can quietly change our behavior too \- and sometimes those changes begin to build on one another.

It is cold, so we skip the walk. It is dark, so we stay home. We are staying home more, so we see fewer people. We feel tired, so we stop doing some of the things we normally enjoy. Then, because we are already feeling a little down, getting dressed and going somewhere begins to feel like even more work.

Before long, several of the things that naturally support our [mental wellbeing](https://arcwellness.net/treatments/brain-health) \- daylight, movement, routine, connection, laughter and simply having something to look forward to \- may have decreased at the same time.

**Don't Hibernate Through The Winter**

There is absolutely nothing wrong with a cozy winter evening at home. In fact, slowing down can be one of the nicest parts of the season. The goal is not to stay constantly busy. The goal is to notice when resting begins turning into retreating.

Try to be intentional about getting daylight when you can. Open the blinds in the morning. Step outside during the middle of the day. Take a short walk when the weather allows. If your favorite outdoor activities are not practical, find ways to keep moving indoors. Walk through the mall, take a class, strength train, stretch, dance around the kitchen or simply put on some music and move.

Just as importantly, keep engaging with people and with life. Meet someone for coffee. Have dinner with your sister. Call a friend. Take the grandkids somewhere. Wander through a bookstore. Cook with someone. Join a class. Go see a movie. Make plans even when staying on the couch sounds easier.

Winter does not have to be packed with activities, but it helps to keep a few things on the calendar that give you a reason to get dressed, leave the house, connect with another person and look forward to something.

**Sometimes We Simply Need Another Human**

There is something about being with people we love that makes life feel a little lighter.

Maybe it is sitting across the table from a friend with a cup of coffee and nowhere you need to be. Maybe it is calling your mom just to hear her voice, laughing with your sister over something that no one else would find funny, or lingering in the kitchen after dinner talking about absolutely nothing important.

Sometimes we don't need advice. We don't need someone to fix anything. We don't even necessarily need to talk about what is bothering us.

Sometimes we simply need another human.

Winter can make those little moments of connection easier to lose. We aren't sitting outside with the neighbors. We don't bump into each other as often. We go from the warm house to the warm car and hurry inside wherever we're going. When it gets dark early and the couch is calling, even making plans for dinner can suddenly feel like an awful lot of effort.

So we have to be a little more intentional about finding one another.

Invite someone over for soup. Meet a friend for breakfast. Take your dog for a walk with a neighbor. Call the person you've been thinking about instead of telling yourself you'll call tomorrow. Sit with your coworker at lunch instead of eating at your desk. Cook dinner with your spouse. Play cards with your parents. Take your grandchild somewhere. Go wander through a bookstore with someone you love.

And don't worry about making it an event.

The house doesn't need to be clean. Dinner doesn't need to be impressive. You don't need the perfect thing to say. There is something wonderfully comforting about simply being in the presence of someone who knows us.

We need that.

We need people who know our stories. People who make us laugh. People we can sit quietly beside. People who notice when we are a little quieter than usual. People who remind us of who we are when we've had a hard week. And sometimes we need to be that person for someone else.

If somebody has been on your mind lately, reach out.

A simple *“I've been thinking about you. Want to have coffee?”* may be exactly what they needed that day. And perhaps exactly what you needed too.

Human connection doesn't make every difficult feeling disappear, and it isn't a substitute for professional support when someone is truly struggling. There are times when sadness becomes more than a passing season—when it persists, begins interfering with everyday life, or brings feelings of hopelessness. Those are moments when reaching out to a healthcare or mental-health professional matters. If you or someone you love is experiencing thoughts of suicide or self-harm, immediate support is available in the United States by calling or texting 988\.

But not every heavy day needs to begin with figuring out what is wrong.

Sometimes we need a warm meal.

Sometimes we need a long conversation.

Sometimes we need to laugh until our stomach hurts.

Sometimes we need someone to sit beside us and say absolutely nothing.

**Sometimes we simply need another human.**

**When Your Brain Could Use a Little Support**

**Sometimes We Need a Little More Support**

Sometimes all of those little things are enough.

A slower pace. More rest. A walk outside when the sun is shining. Dinner with someone we love. Getting back into a routine. Finding reasons to laugh. Giving ourselves permission to have a quieter season without feeling as though we need to be productive every minute of it.

And sometimes we do all of those things and still don't quite feel like ourselves.

That matters too.

At [Arc Wellness](https://arcwellness.net/about), we don't believe every difficult season needs to be turned into a diagnosis. We also don't believe you should have to wait until something feels truly wrong before asking for support. There is a lot of space between feeling wonderful and feeling unwell, and many of us spend periods of our lives somewhere in that middle.

Maybe you're sleeping, but you don't wake feeling restored. Maybe your [energy isn't what it normally is](https://arcwellness.net/blogs/why-youre-tired-even-when-your-labs-are-normal). You feel [more stressed or overwhelmed](https://arcwellness.net/blogs/nervous-system-dysregulation-symptoms-causes-healing). Your motivation has disappeared. Your mind feels cluttered. You aren't enjoying things quite as much. Or perhaps you can't put your finger on anything specific—you simply know that you don't feel quite like *you*.

That is [a perfectly reasonable place to begin a conversation](https://arcwellness.net/contact).

One of the technologies available at Arc is [**ExoMind**](https://arcwellness.net/treatments/exomind), a noninvasive treatment that uses magnetic stimulation to influence targeted areas of the brain. Depending on the individual and the reason for treatment, ExoMind may be considered as one part of a broader approach to supporting [mental and cognitive wellness](https://arcwellness.net/treatments/brain-health).

Mental wellness is connected to how we sleep, how we move, how we feel physically, how connected we are to other people, how much stress we're carrying, whether we're making time for ourselves and whether our days still contain things that bring us pleasure.

That's why support doesn't always look the same for everyone.

For one person, it may be ExoMind. For another, it may be [getting stronger again](https://arcwellness.net/treatments/emsculpt-neo). Someone else may need help sleeping through the night. Another person may simply need an hour devoted entirely to themself. And sometimes what someone needs is outside of what we provide at Arc—and that's important to recognize too. There are times when the right support is a counselor, therapist, psychiatrist or another healthcare professional.

The goal isn't to find a treatment for winter.

**The goal is to help you continue feeling like yourself through all of life's seasons.**

So if winter arrives and you find yourself feeling a little different, give yourself some grace. Rest. Find your people. Move when your body needs movement and be still when it needs stillness.

And if you need a little more support along the way, it's okay to ask for it.

**And when you notice your world beginning to get smaller, intentionally step back into it.**

***The trees may become bare and the days may become shorter,***  
***but that doesn't mean our lives have to become smaller with them.***

**Frequently Asked Questions**

**Why do shorter days affect mood?**

Light plays an important role in the body's internal clock, including the rhythms that help regulate sleep and wakefulness. As daylight decreases, some people notice changes in energy, sleep, appetite, motivation and mood.

**What is seasonal affective disorder (SAD)?**

For some people, seasonal changes become significant enough to develop a seasonal pattern of depression, often referred to as seasonal affective disorder, or SAD. For many others, winter may simply bring feeling more tired, less motivated or more withdrawn than usual.

**How can I support my mood during the winter months?**

Get daylight when you can by opening the blinds in the morning and stepping outside during the middle of the day. Keep moving indoors when outdoor activities are not practical, and keep a few plans on the calendar that give you a reason to leave the house, connect with someone and look forward to something.

**Why does staying connected with people matter in winter?**

Cold weather and early darkness make it easier to stay home and see fewer people. Simple time with people who know us, like coffee with a friend, a phone call or dinner together, can make life feel a little lighter. Connection is not a substitute for professional support when someone is truly struggling.
